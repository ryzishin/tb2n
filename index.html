The Foundation

Genetics & Heredity

Before any Punnett square makes sense, you need a clear picture of what genetics actually studies and how heredity moves information from one generation to the next. Everything else in this unit — Mendel's laws, codominance, sex-linked traits — is just a refinement of these two ideas.

What is genetics?

Genetics is the branch of biology that studies heredity — the way traits and characteristics are passed from parents to offspring through genes. A gene is a segment of DNA that codes for a specific protein, and that protein eventually builds or regulates some part of the body: an enzyme, a pigment, a clotting factor, a receptor. Different versions of the same gene are called alleles, and they are the reason two humans with the same gene can look, behave, or risk disease very differently.

The complete set of genes an organism carries is its genome. Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes packed onto 23 pairs of chromosomes — 46 DNA molecules total in nearly every cell of your body. Genetics as a field did not begin with DNA, though. It began with a 19th-century monk counting peas in a monastery garden, and his patient arithmetic is still the foundation of every cross you will see in this unit.

What is heredity?

Heredity is the process genetics studies — the actual transmission of traits from parent to child. When a sperm and an egg fuse, each parent contributes one chromosome from every pair, so the offspring ends up with a shuffled, half-and-half genome. This reshuffling is why siblings (except identical twins) are never genetically the same, even though they share the same parents.

Not every trait is inherited in the same neat way. Some follow Gregor Mendel's clean dominant/recessive ratios (seed shape, blood type antigens, hemophilia). Others blend or share the stage — incomplete dominance produces an intermediate phenotype, codominance lets both alleles show at once, and sex-linked traits ride on the X chromosome. The same logic — alleles segregating into gametes and recombining at fertilization — drives all of them, which is why the Punnett square is the single most useful tool in this entire unit.

Vocabulary you need

Gene

A stretch of DNA that codes for one protein or RNA — the basic unit of heredity. Example: the F8 gene codes for clotting factor VIII.

Allele

One alternative version of a gene. You inherit one allele from each parent — they may be identical or different.

Genotype

The actual allele pair an organism carries, written like Aa or XHXh. It is the genetic recipe — what the DNA literally says.

Phenotype

What you can actually observe — round seeds, type A blood, prolonged bleeding, color vision. The phenotype is the genotype's visible result.

Homozygous

Both alleles are the same — AA (homozygous dominant) or aa (homozygous recessive). The trait is "true-breeding".

Heterozygous

The two alleles differ — Aa. The organism carries one copy of each version and is also called a hybrid for that trait.

Dominant

The allele that is expressed in the heterozygote. Written with a capital letter (A). Does not mean stronger, better, or more common — it just wins the visible contest.

Recessive

The allele that is hidden in the heterozygote and only shows when homozygous. Written lowercase (a).

Why a Punnett square works

A Punnett square is a 2×2 (or 4×4) grid that lists every possible combination of parental gametes. Each parent can pass only one of its two alleles per gene to a child — that is Mendel's Law of Segregation in one sentence. So a heterozygous Aa parent makes two kinds of gametes in equal numbers: A and a. Cross two Aa parents and the four cells of the square are AA, Aa, Aa, aa — a 3 : 1 phenotype ratio and a 1 : 2 : 1 genotype ratio.

That is the whole engine of heredity in a single picture. The rest of the unit just changes which letters go in the square, what "dominant" means, or which chromosome carries the gene.

How to read the symbols

  • A / a dominant / recessive alleles of an autosomal gene (peas, blood type antigens, etc.)
  • XH / Xh alleles riding on the X chromosome — used for hemophilia
  • XC / Xc same idea for color vision: normal vs. colorblind
  • ♀ / ♂ read as "female / male"
  • P / F1 / F2 parental, first filial, second filial generations

Capital letters are not always dominant in non-Mendelian crosses — for codominance and incomplete dominance, both alleles are often written as capitals with different superscripts (e.g. IAIB for blood type AB).

The Father of Genetics

Mendel's Three Laws

Between 1856 and 1863, an Augustinian friar named Gregor Mendel tracked roughly 28,000 pea plants in a monastery garden in Brno. By crossing plants that differed in one clear trait at a time — seed shape, flower color, pod color — he distilled heredity into three laws that still bear his name and still explain every Punnett square you will draw.

Law 1

Law of Dominance

One allele can mask the other.

When two different alleles are present in the same organism, only one — the dominant allele — shows up in the phenotype. The other, the recessive allele, is hidden but still there, ready to reappear in a later generation. Mendel saw this when he crossed a true-breeding tall pea (TT) with a true-breeding short pea (tt): every F1 offspring was tall, even though the short allele had not vanished — it was simply being masked.

This law is the reason a single visible trait does not tell you the genotype. A tall pea might be TT or Tt; you cannot tell by looking. Only a test cross — breeding it with a homozygous recessive — would reveal the truth.

Worked example

TT (tall) × tt (short) → 100% Tt (tall in appearance, but carriers of t)

Law 2

Law of Segregation

Two alleles split up when gametes form.

Every organism carries two alleles for each gene — one from each parent. When that organism makes sperm or eggs, the two alleles separate (segregate) so that each gamete receives only one of them. Which allele a gamete gets is a 50/50 coin flip. Fertilization then reunites one allele from each parent, restoring the pair in the offspring.

This is the law that justifies the existence of the Punnett square. A heterozygous Tt plant makes 50% T gametes and 50% t gametes — so the four cells of a Tt × Tt square represent real, equal-probability outcomes.

Worked example — monohybrid cross

Tt × Tt → 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt

Phenotype ratio: 3 tall : 1 short (the recessive 25% reappears in F2 because the alleles segregated during gamete formation).

Law 3

Law of Independent Assortment

Different genes sort independently — when on different chromosomes.

Alleles of different genes are passed to gametes independently of one another. The allele a pea passes on for seed shape has no influence on which allele it passes on for seed color — so an RrYy plant makes four equally likely gamete types: RY, Ry, rY, ry. Cross two of them and you get the famous 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio of the F2 dihybrid.

This law holds only when the two genes sit on different chromosomes (or are very far apart on the same one). Genes that are close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together — a phenomenon called linkage — and that is the one big exception Mendel's peas happened to dodge.

Worked example — dihybrid cross

RrYy × RrYy → 9 R_Y_ (round yellow) : 3 R_yy (round green) : 3 rrY_ (wrinkled yellow) : 1 rryy (wrinkled green)

Why Mendel succeeded where others failed

Three habits made Mendel's experiments work where previous breeders had only produced muddles. First, he chose true-breeding lines — plants that always produced offspring identical to themselves, so he knew his starting genotypes were homozygous. Second, he tracked one trait at a time, controlling the variables instead of letting everything mix at once. Third, he counted — 7,324 peas in one experiment — and noticed that the ratios (3:1, 9:3:3:1) were not random but mathematical. That quantitative eye is what turned gardening into genetics.

When Mendel's Rules Break

Non-Mendelian Inheritance

Not every allele is a clean winner-loser pair. In incomplete dominance the heterozygote looks like a blend of the two parents; in codominance both alleles are fully and separately visible at the same time. The Punnett square still works — only the phenotype labels change.

Incomplete Dominance — the "blended" phenotype

In incomplete dominance, neither allele is truly dominant. The heterozygote ends up with a phenotype that is intermediate between the two homozygous parents — it looks "blended", even though no blending has actually happened at the DNA level. The alleles still segregate cleanly; what is blended is the amount of pigment or protein they produce.

The classic textbook case is snapdragon flower color. A red snapdragon (R R) crossed with a white snapdragon (r r) produces F1 offspring that are pink (Rr) — neither parent's color, but a halfway shade. Cross two pinks and the F2 ratio is 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The genotype ratio (1 : 2 : 1) is identical to a Mendelian monohybrid cross — only the phenotype ratio changed, because the heterozygote is now visibly different from either homozygote.

Real human example: hypercholesterolemia. A person with one normal LDL-receptor allele and one faulty allele has cholesterol levels roughly twice normal — between the unaffected homozygote (normal) and the affected homozygote (5–6× normal). One working copy of the gene is not quite enough to keep up with the body's demand.

Codominance — both alleles show at once

In codominance, both alleles are fully and separately expressed in the heterozygote — not blended, but both present side by side. If incomplete dominance is mixing red and white paint to get pink, codominance is painting one half of the flower red and the other half white. Each allele does its job; neither masks the other.

The classic case is human ABO blood type. The A allele (IA) makes A antigens on red blood cells; the B allele (IB) makes B antigens. A person with genotype IAIB has both A and B antigens on every red blood cell — blood type AB. Neither is dominant, neither is hidden, and the result is not a "medium" antigen but two fully functional ones. Cross an IAIA parent with an IBIB parent and 100% of the children will be type AB.

Another textbook case is sickle-cell trait. A person with one normal hemoglobin allele (HbA) and one sickle allele (HbS) makes both kinds of hemoglobin in the same red blood cells. They are generally healthy (some cells sickle under low oxygen) but they carry both alleles — codominance at the molecular level even though clinically the trait is described as "recessive".

Quick way to tell them apart

If the heterozygote looks in between the two parents (a blend, a midpoint, a halfway shade), it is incomplete dominance. If the heterozygote shows both parental traits fully and separately at the same time (both antigens, both pigments, both hemoglobins), it is codominance. Same Punnett square, same 1:2:1 genotype ratio — the difference is what the heterozygote looks like.

A Special Case of Mendel

Sex-Linked Inheritance 101

Almost every trait you have studied so far was autosomal — the gene sat on one of the 22 pairs of non-sex chromosomes, so males and females inherited it the same way. Sex-linked traits break that symmetry.

What "sex-linked" means

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes plus one pair of sex chromosomes. Females are XX and males are XY. A trait is sex-linked when its gene sits on a sex chromosome — and in practice, nearly every famous sex-linked disorder is X-linked, because the X carries roughly 900 genes while the tiny Y carries only about 70, mostly for sex determination and male fertility.

Hemophilia A, hemophilia B, and red–green colorblindness are all X-linked recessive traits. "Recessive" means one working copy of the gene is enough to stay healthy — so a female with one normal allele and one faulty allele usually shows no symptoms. She is a carrier. A male has no such safety net, which is the whole secret of why these disorders skip girls and land on boys.

Who gives which chromosome?

The rules of X-linked recessive

  1. A female must inherit the recessive allele from both parents to be affected.
  2. A male is affected by a single recessive X — there is no second copy to compensate.
  3. Fathers pass their X only to daughters and their Y only to sons — so there is no father-to-son transmission of X-linked traits.
  4. A carrier mother has a 50% chance of passing the recessive X to each child, whatever the child's sex.
  5. Every cell of a Punnett square equals a 25% chance per pregnancy.

How to read the symbols

  • XH X chromosome carrying the normal (dominant) clotting allele
  • Xh X chromosome carrying the recessive hemophilia allele
  • XC / Xc same idea for color vision: normal vs. colorblind
  • Y carries no allele for these traits — it just decides maleness
  • ♀ / ♂ read as "female / male"

Superscript letters are alleles riding on the X. If the superscript is capitalized, the allele works. Lowercase = broken.

Case Study 1

Hemophilia A & B

Hemophilia is a bleeding disorder where blood does not clot properly, because one of the clotting factor genes on the X chromosome is faulty. Cuts and bruises that others shrug off can turn into long bleeds for a person with hemophilia.

Symptoms

  • Easy, deep bruising from small bumps
  • Prolonged bleeding after cuts, dental work, or surgery
  • Spontaneous nosebleeds
  • Bleeding into joints & muscles → pain, swelling, stiffness

Severity levels

  • Mild — 5–40% of normal factor activity
  • Moderate — 1–5%
  • Severe — less than 1%, bleeds without injury

About 30% of cases come from brand-new (de novo) mutations — no family history at all.

Treatment

  • Infusion of the missing clotting factor (VIII or IX)
  • Preventive infusions for severe cases
  • Gene therapy now approved for both types

With treatment, most patients live nearly normal lives.

History: "the royal disease"

Queen Victoria of Britain was a carrier of hemophilia (believed to be type B). She passed the recessive X through several of her daughters into the royal families of Spain, Germany, and Russia — most famously to her great-grandson Alexei, son of Tsar Nicholas II. One carrier granddaughter, Alice, married into the Russian court, and the disease rippled across 19th-century European royalty. It remains biology's most famous pedigree.

Case Study 2

Red–Green Colorblindness

Red–green color vision deficiency is the most common X-linked disorder in humans. The genes for the red (OPN1LW) and green (OPN1MW) light-sensing pigments sit side by side at the tip of the X chromosome (Xq28) — so close together that they sometimes get mixed up when eggs or sperm are made.

Why it's so common in males

A male with one faulty pigment gene on his only X chromosome is colorblind — end of story, since his Y carries no backup copy. A female needs the faulty gene on both X chromosomes, which requires an affected or colorblind father and at least a carrier mother. That double requirement is why only about 1 in 200 females is red–green colorblind, while roughly 1 in 12 males is — a 24-fold difference.

People with the condition usually see reds, greens, browns, and oranges as muddled shades of the same color. It is usually mild enough to go unnoticed for years — many discover it during school vision screening or when a red traffic light "disappears" among streetlights.

Before the Exam

Key Takeaways

  1. Genetics studies heredity. Heredity is the transmission of traits via alleles — different versions of the same gene — that segregate into gametes and recombine at fertilization.
  2. Mendel's three laws: dominance (one allele can mask another), segregation (two alleles split during gamete formation), and independent assortment (genes on different chromosomes sort independently → 9:3:3:1 in dihybrid F2).
  3. Not everything is Mendelian. Incomplete dominance → a blended heterozygote (snapdragons: red × white = pink). Codominance → both alleles fully expressed (blood type AB, sickle-cell trait).
  4. Sex-linked traits ride on the X. Males are hemizygous — one X, no backup — so X-linked recessive disorders hit males far more often than females.
  5. Carriers are females. XHXh or XCXc — usually healthy, 50% chance of passing the recessive X to each child.
  6. No father-to-son transmission. Dad gives sons a Y, not an X. If a trait appears in a son, the recessive allele came from Mom.
  7. Probabilities, not promises. Each cell is a 25% chance per pregnancy — real families can absolutely beat the odds in either direction.

Exam quick hits

  • Mono F2: 3:1 phenotype · 1:2:1 genotype
  • Dihybrid F2: 9:3:3:1 phenotype
  • Incomplete dominance F2: 1:2:1 phenotype (same as genotype)
  • Codominance (ABO): IAIB = type AB, both antigens present
  • Factor VIII = Hemophilia A · Factor IX = Hemophilia B
  • Colorblindness: 1 in 12 males vs. 1 in 200 females — 24× difference
  • Males can never be carriers of X-linked recessive traits

Reading a pedigree — 30-second check

  • MANY affected males, few/no affected females → think X-linked recessive
  • Affected dad with unaffected kids → look for carrier daughters
  • Affected woman → all her sons affected, all daughters at least carriers
  • Three phenotype classes in F2 with 1:2:1 ratio → incomplete dominance
  • Both parental phenotypes reappearing in heterozygote → codominance
